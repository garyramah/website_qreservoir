The Quantum Bell

A quantum reservoir computer is a bell you never teach to ring. Strike it with a strength that encodes your data, listen to how it rings — the spectrum of photon-number probabilities — and let a simple classical layer learn which ringing patterns mean what. The quantum part is never trained; only the listener is. Everything below is live: the bell on this page is a real (simplified) simulation of a driven, dissipative quantum resonator.

What you hear — the Fock spectrum P(n)

Each bar is one "neuron": the probability of finding n photons. For a pure coherent state this is a Poisson distribution in n; Kerr nonlinearity and dissipation distort it. Listening at several instants (time-multiplexing) multiplies the features.

The listener: a classical readout that learns

The bell is never trained. The only learned part is a linear map — ridge regression — from the ringing features to a label. Here is the classic test that shows why the reservoir earns its keep: parity. Five pulses arrive in sequence (1 = strike, 0 = silence); the label is whether the number of strikes is odd. Parity is mathematically invisible to any linear readout of the raw pulses — such a readout grades at chance level, as the control below confirms. But a linear readout of the ringing can learn it, because the resonator has already mixed the pulses nonlinearly and holds them, fading, in its field.

Train the readout — ridge regression, solved on this page

Training rings 120 random 5-pulse sequences through the simulated resonator, sampling the ringing at seven instants — after every pulse, and twice more as it dies away. Each instant contributes P(0)…P(5), ⟨n⟩, and the quadratures ⟨a⟩ and ⟨a²⟩: 78 features. Ridge regression solves for the weights, and grading happens on 60 fresh sequences the readout never trained on. It uses your current K and κ — change them and retrain.

The analogy, mapped precisely

Each element of the bell corresponds to a specific piece of the quantum-resonator reservoir.

The bell the resonator — cavity / bosonic mode

A physical oscillator with a ladder of energy levels — the Fock states |0⟩, |1⟩, |2⟩, … Driven, it "rings" according to the quantum dynamics of the driven, dissipative oscillator, including any Kerr nonlinearity inherited from a coupled qubit.

H = −(K/2)·a†a†aa + drive,   loss at rate κ

The strike input data — coherent drive amplitude

Input data is encoded in the amplitude of the drive that strikes — or continuously drives — the bell. A stronger or weaker coherent pulse displaces the cavity field further or less, exactly as different striking strengths produce different ringing amplitudes and overtone content.

u ∈ [0,1]  →  drive ε(t) ∝ u  →  displacement α

The ringing you hear measured Fock-state probabilities

The outputs are the measured occupation probabilities of the Fock states (or other observables such as quadratures) at one or more times after the drive. The distribution over photon numbers is a nonlinear function of the drive amplitude — Poissonian for a pure coherent state, further distorted by Kerr nonlinearity and dissipation — so each Fock probability acts as a different nonlinear "neuron." Time-multiplexing, listening at several instants, gives more features from the same ringing event.

features x = [ P(n, t₁), P(n, t₂), … ]  ·  coherent state: P(n) = e^(−λ) λⁿ / n!

The listener classical output layer — linear map

A simple linear map — usually ridge regression or ordinary least-squares — takes the vector of measured probabilities and learns the weights that best map it onto the target label or value assigned to each input sequence.

ŷ = w·x + b  ·  the only trained part of the machine

What is actually being simulated

The wavefunction ψ lives in the Fock basis, truncated at n = 17, and is integrated live (Runge–Kutta) in the frame rotating at the drive frequency: a coherent drive displaces the field, the Kerr term −(K/2)a†a†aa rotates each Fock component at an n-dependent rate, and photon loss damps each component at rate κn/2. For clarity the simulation follows the pure-state (no-jump) evolution, so treat fine details as illustrative rather than lab-grade — the qualitative physics (Poisson ringing, Kerr shear, fading memory, nonlinear features) is the real dynamics.

This is precisely the architecture demonstrated with a single transmon coupled to a superconducting coplanar-waveguide resonator, and it is the conceptual core of the coupled-oscillator reservoir proposals: you hit the quantum bell with a strength that encodes your data, you listen to how it rings, and a classical linear layer learns which ringing patterns correspond to which labels.

In short: the quantum part never needs to be trained — only the final classical mapping does. That is the whole economy of reservoir computing, and the bell is its oldest intuition: the instrument is fixed; the ear is what learns.
